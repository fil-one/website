# AI writing tropes

Prose written by an LLM has a recognizable accent. Readers may not name the pattern, but they notice it, and once they do they discount the content. This skill lists the tells and how to fix them.

## How to use this

Writing something new: read the catalog first, then write. Do not write a draft and clean it up after, because the tropes below are what fluent-sounding filler collapses into by default, and a cleanup pass usually just relabels them.

Editing an existing draft: scan for the categories in order (word choice, sentence structure, paragraph structure, tone, formatting, composition). Formatting and composition problems are the fastest to spot and the most visible to readers, so start there if the draft is long.

## The judgment call

Every item below is a real rhetorical device that good writers use. One em dash is punctuation. One "it's not X, it's Y" is emphasis. The tell is density: several tropes in one paragraph, or one trope repeated down the page. A human writer reaches for a device when a specific sentence needs it; an LLM reaches for it every third sentence because it is the shape of confident prose.

So do not enforce this as a banned-words list. Ask instead: is this device doing work here that a plainer sentence would not do? If yes, keep it. If it is there for rhythm or emphasis-by-default, cut it.

The most common failure when applying this skill is over-correction — stripping out all rhythm and voice and producing flat, clipped prose. That reads as bad writing rather than as human writing. Aim for a draft that sounds like a specific person with a point of view, not one that sounds like it passed a filter.

## Word choice

Magic adverbs. "Quietly," "deeply," "fundamentally," "seamlessly," "carefully" attached to verbs that do not need them: "quietly orchestrating workflows, decisions, and interactions" / "the one that quietly suffocates everything else." The adverb implies significance the sentence has not earned. Cut it, or replace it with the concrete fact that made you reach for it.

LLM vocabulary. "Delve," "leverage," "robust," "streamline," "harness," "utilize," "underscore," "myriad." These are not forbidden words, but they cluster in machine prose because they sound formal without committing to meaning. "Let's delve into the details" is "here are the details." "Leverage these robust frameworks" is "use these frameworks."

Grand nouns. "Tapestry," "landscape," "paradigm," "ecosystem," "realm," "journey": "the rich tapestry of human experience," "navigating the complex landscape of modern AI," "the ever-evolving landscape of technology." Name the actual thing. The landscape of X is usually just X.

The "serves as" dodge. Replacing "is" with "serves as," "stands as," "marks," "represents": "the building serves as a reminder," "the station marks a pivotal moment." It inflates a plain statement into a ceremonial one. Use "is."

## Sentence structure

Negative parallelism. "It's not X, it's Y." Variants: "not because X, but because Y," "X, not Y," and the cross-sentence version "The question isn't X. The question is Y." Fine once in a piece when you are genuinely correcting a misconception the reader holds. As a default rhythm it manufactures contrast that is not there.

"Not X. Not Y. Just Z." The escalating countdown: "Not a bug. Not a feature. A fundamental design flaw." / "Not ten. Not fifty. Five hundred and twenty-three lint violations." State the number.

"The X? A Y." Self-answered mini-questions: "The result? Devastating." / "The worst part? Nobody saw it coming." One per piece is a stylistic tic; three is a signature.

Anaphora abuse. Consecutive sentences opening the same way: "They assume that... They could expose... They have built..." Parallel openings are for building to something. If nothing is being built to, vary the openings.

Tricolon abuse. Everything arriving in threes: "workflows, decisions, and interactions" / "identity, payments, compute, distribution" / "products impress people; platforms empower them." Real lists are rarely exactly three items long. If the third item is padding, use two.

"It's worth noting." Also "importantly," "interestingly," "notably," "it's important to remember." These announce significance instead of demonstrating it, and they are always deletable without loss. Delete them.

Trailing "-ing" significance. Sentences that end by explaining their own importance: "...highlighting its importance," "...reflecting broader trends," "...contributing to the development of." The reader can see the significance, or the sentence has not made the case and an appended participle will not fix it.

False ranges. "From X to Y" where the endpoints are not opposite ends of any real spectrum: "from innovation to implementation to cultural transformation." If the range does not span anything, it is a list — write it as one.

## Paragraph structure

Short punchy fragments. Sentence fragments standing alone for emphasis: "He published this. Openly. In a book. As a priest." One fragment lands. A page of them reads as a drumroll with no drums, and it destroys the ability to actually emphasize anything.

Listicle in a trench coat. A list rewritten as prose but keeping the enumeration: "The first wall is... The second takeaway is... The third thing to understand..." If it is a list, format it as a list. If it is an argument, let each paragraph follow from the last instead of being numbered.

## Tone

"Here's the kicker." Also "here's the thing," "here's where it gets interesting," "here's what most people miss." False suspense promising a revelation that turns out to be ordinary. If the next sentence is genuinely surprising, it does not need the announcement.

"Think of it as..." Patronizing analogies: "think of it like a highway system for data," "it's like a Swiss Army knife." Use an analogy when the concept is genuinely unfamiliar to this specific reader, and pick one that carries real detail. Otherwise explain the thing directly.

"Imagine a world where..." Invitations to futurism followed by a list of pleasant outcomes: "imagine a world where every tool you use has quiet intelligence behind it." Describe what the thing does now.

False vulnerability. Performed candor: "I'm openly in love with the platform model," "this is not a rant; it's a diagnosis." Signaling honesty is not the same as being specific, and readers can tell.

"The truth is simple." Also "the reality is simpler," "history is unambiguous on this point." Asserting that a claim is obvious in place of supporting it. Support it.

Stakes inflation. "This will fundamentally reshape everything," "will define the next era of computing." When everything is world-historic, nothing is. Say what actually changes and for whom.

"Let's break this down." Also "let's unpack this," "let's explore," "let's dive in." Pedagogical hand-holding. Start explaining.

Vague attributions. "Experts argue," "industry reports suggest," "observers have cited," "studies show." Name the source or drop the claim. An unnamed authority is a made-up one, and this is the trope most likely to be an actual factual problem rather than a stylistic one.

Invented concept labels. Coining compound terms that sound analytical without being defined: "supervision paradox," "acceleration trap," "workload creep." Naming a phenomenon is not explaining it. If you coin a term, earn it by defining it and using it consistently; otherwise describe the phenomenon.

## Formatting

Em-dash addiction. Em dashes used for every pause, aside, and pivot: "the problem — and this is the part nobody talks about — is systemic." Most can be commas, parentheses, a colon, or a full stop. Keep the ones where the interruption is the point.

Bold-first bullets. Every list item opening with a bolded label: "**Security**: environment-based configuration..." / "**Performance**: lazy loading of..." Fine for genuine key-value reference material. As the default shape of every list, it turns prose into a slide deck. Let bullets be sentences.

Unicode decoration. Arrows (→) in place of words, decorative symbols, smart quotes in code or plain-text contexts. "Input → Processing → Output" is a diagram pretending to be a sentence.

## Composition

Fractal summaries. "What I'm going to tell you, what I'm telling you, what I just told you," applied at the document, section, and paragraph level. One summary per document, at most.

The dead metaphor. Introducing a metaphor and then returning to it five or ten times ("the highway," "the engine," "the foundation") until it is doing more work than the argument. Use it once and move on.

Historical analogy stacking. Rapid-fire references to past companies or technology shifts (railroads, electricity, the browser wars, mobile) to borrow authority. One analogy examined closely beats five gestured at.

One-point dilution. A single argument restated ten ways across thousands of words. If the piece has one idea, it is a short piece.

Content duplication. Whole sentences or sections repeated near-verbatim in the same document. This is the clearest tell of all, and it is worth a dedicated scan on long drafts.

The signposted conclusion. "In conclusion," "to sum up," "in summary." End with the actual ending.

"Despite its challenges..." The concession formula that raises an objection only to dismiss it in the same breath: "despite its challenges, the technology remains promising." Either engage the objection or leave it out.

## Revising a draft

Work in this order, because each pass makes the next one easier to see.

1. Composition and formatting. Delete duplicated content, the signposted conclusion, and repeated summaries. Convert bold-first bullet stacks to sentences. Count em dashes: if there is more than roughly one per few paragraphs, most are load-bearing for nothing.
2. Tone. Remove the suspense builders, the pedagogical openers, and the stakes inflation. Replace vague attributions with named sources or cut the claims.
3. Sentence structure. Find repeated shapes: negative parallelism, self-answered questions, consecutive same-shaped openings, everything in threes. Vary or cut, keeping at most one instance of each device where it earns its place.
4. Word choice. Last, because by now much of the affected text is gone. Cut magic adverbs, swap "serves as" for "is," and replace grand nouns with the specific thing.

Then read it aloud. The remaining tells are usually audible before they are visible.

## When the user asks for a diagnosis

If the ask is "why does this read like AI," do not just apply fixes. Quote the specific lines and name the trope, so the writer can recognize the pattern in their own future drafts. A rewrite without the diagnosis teaches nothing.
